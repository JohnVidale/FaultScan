# FaultScan Science Goals

## Ultimate objective

FaultScan's ultimate scientific goal is to search for systematic changes in
and around the S wave that reveal time-dependent structure at depth near the
San Jacinto fault zone. The experiment asks whether the subsurface changed
across an M3.4 mainshock and during the first few hours of its aftershock
sequence.

## Earthquake sequence

The target sequence consists of an M3.4 mainshock, a few foreshocks, and a set
of aftershocks during the following few hours. The earthquakes have highly
repeatable waveforms and are nearly identical apart from magnitude. Clipping
is not expected, and relative magnitude can be estimated and removed through
amplitude normalization.

For the smaller earthquakes, the source-time function is effectively a delta
function at the timescales of interest. The larger events have slightly longer
source durations, so changes in the detailed shape of the mainshock S arrival
must be distinguished from propagation effects. The small foreshocks and
aftershocks provide the cleanest repeat-source comparisons, while the M3.4
mainshock defines the possible structural perturbation and temporal boundary.

## Observational geometry

The primary receiver is an approximately 300-element, three-component array
located about 10 km from earthquakes that are roughly 10 km deep. Coherent
stacking across this array provides much higher S-wave signal-to-noise ratio
than individual regional stations and is the main tool for detecting subtle
waveform changes. The three components permit comparison of radial,
transverse, and vertical motion, as well as changes in polarization, splitting,
and scattered energy.

Data are also available from about 10 individual stations surrounding the
source region. Although their S-wave signal-to-noise ratios are lower, their
azimuthal coverage is important for precise relative relocation and for
testing whether a signal is localized near the source, confined to the
source-to-array path, or caused by an individual receiver.

## Controlling for earthquake location

Differences in hypocentral location are a central uncertainty because even
small offsets can change takeoff angle, radiation amplitude, and the sampled
near-source path. Mingze Du's relative relocations currently show that the
events colocate within approximately 500--1000 m, while their absolute
locations are less well constrained.

The surrounding stations allow relative locations to be refined using
cross-correlation differential times from the highly repeatable waveforms.
P-wave measurements are especially useful because the target signal is near
the S wave; S-wave differential times can add information but must be checked
for bias if the S-wave propagation medium changed. Absolute location is most
important for associating the sequence with a particular fault strand or
structure, whereas precise relative locations are essential for separating
spatial waveform differences from temporal changes.

The cleanest tests will compare event pairs that are as close as possible in
relative location but occur on opposite sides of the mainshock. More broadly,
the analysis should test whether waveform differences correlate more strongly
with event time or with relative hypocentral position.

## Relationship to the San Jacinto fault

The relocated earthquakes follow a trend parallel to the San Jacinto fault
and lie within approximately 500 m of its mapped trace. The M3.4 mainshock has
a predominantly strike-slip focal mechanism, with one nodal plane aligned
with the local fault trace. The agreement among the hypocentral trend, event
proximity, mapped fault geometry, and focal mechanism favors the working
hypothesis that this sequence ruptured the San Jacinto fault itself.

This interpretation is not unique because the absolute earthquake locations
are less well constrained and a focal mechanism does not by itself identify
which nodal plane is the physical fault plane. Nevertheless, the independent
geometric observations favor the fault-parallel plane. Consequently, a
time-dependent S-wave change associated with the mainshock could represent
evolution within or immediately adjacent to the active San Jacinto fault zone,
rather than merely a change somewhere along a more distant propagation path.

## Target observations

After correcting for event location, origin time, station statics, amplitude,
and the modest source-duration differences of the larger events, the analysis
will search for changes in:

- Differential S-wave arrival time.
- Waveform phase, shape, and inter-event coherence.
- Amplitude and frequency content.
- Polarization and shear-wave splitting.
- The early S coda and scattered-wave energy.
- Spatial patterns across the dense array and among subarrays.

The primary comparison is the evolution from foreshocks through the M3.4
mainshock to successive aftershocks. Of particular interest are changes that
appear abruptly across the mainshock and then persist, grow, or recover during
the following hours.

## Criteria for a convincing structural signal

A credible time-dependent fault-zone signal should:

- Remain after corrections for relative earthquake location and origin time.
- Be inconsistent with simple magnitude scaling or the longer source duration
  of the larger events.
- Be coherent across many array elements or show a physically interpretable
  spatial pattern.
- Correlate with time relative to the mainshock more strongly than with event
  position.
- Be supported, where signal quality permits, by observations from the
  surrounding stations or by consistent behavior on multiple components.

Such a signal could indicate evolving stress, cracks, fluids, damage, or other
changes in the elastic or scattering properties of the fault-zone volume.

## Role of the current processing work

Station-static estimation, event alignment, component rotation, waveform
screening, and high-precision relocation are enabling steps rather than the
final scientific result. Their purpose is to suppress timing, location, site,
instrument, and processing artifacts sufficiently that small temporal changes
near the S wave can be interpreted physically.
